During my junior year at BUET, together with a teammate, I designed an electric-powered passenger vessel for Bangladesh. At the time, electric marine transportation was largely unexplored in the country. But I was motivated by the potential of sustainable technologies to reduce environmental impacts in inland waterways. It also exposed me to a practical challenge that later shaped my research direction: the vessel's most persistent enemy was not its engine, but its hull. A slow, invisible creep of hull biofouling increased drag, raised fuel consumption, and shortened maintenance cycles. That single observation reshaped my research agenda. If I could teach a machine to classify what human inspectors struggle to locate reliably, I could convert a maintenance problem into an environmental solution.

I had no formal background in computer science. I taught myself deep learning from scratch through self-directed study of research literature. For months, I ran training experiments on Colab and Kaggle GPUs late into the night, learning to read loss curves the way a mechanic listens to an engine. The effort culminated in a **YOLOv8-based biofouling and corrosion segmentation system** for my undergraduate thesis, using a dataset I physically collected and curated from **Narayanganj Dockyard**. The project received the **RISE Student Research Grant**, providing formal recognition that this self-directed work had scientific merit.

However, successfully defending my thesis was the moment my real research began. The system worked, but a nagging bottleneck remained: with only 35 high-resolution hull images, every performance metric was highly sensitive to how augmentations were ordered and combined. This is a fundamental challenge for any vision system deployed in data-scarce industrial environments. Rather than moving on, I stayed with the problem after graduation. I spent months designing a structured experimental framework, analyzing 110 pairwise augmentation combinations to develop an **Interaction-Effect metric** and a **k–n Fold Augmentation Cross-Validation protocol**. Partway through, I consulted Dr. Hoque and Samiul Based Shuvo for methodological review, and they are co-authors in recognition of their feedback on the final experimental design. Working on this manuscript purely out of intellectual compulsion, without institutional pressure, while tutoring students to support myself taught me that I do not need external structure to do rigorous work. I simply need the right environment and collaborators.

My previous research focused entirely on enabling machines to perceive their environment. This experience sharpened my understanding of what applied computer vision can and cannot yet do reliably on its own. Applied perception is only one component of a holistic monitoring system; to create practical engineering solutions, my perception pipelines must **seamlessly integrate with physical autonomous platforms**. I am arriving at graduate school with a demonstrated ability to execute rigorous, self-directed applied research under **extreme data scarcity**, imperfect operating conditions, and real deployment constraints.

My goal is to transition my **data-efficient vision models to process raw, unstructured sensor feeds from UAV, ROV, and autonomous field platforms**, directly supporting intelligent infrastructure inspection, marine asset integrity, and hazard mitigation. Ultimately, I aspire to become a research scientist developing technologies that address real-world physical bottlenecks while supporting the transition toward smarter, more resilient engineering systems.
