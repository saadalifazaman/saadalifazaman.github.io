My fascination with engineering began long before university. During middle school, I actively participated in mathematics and science olympiads. There I discovered the excitement of solving challenging problems and exploring ideas beyond the classroom. This curiosity eventually led me to pursue engineering at BUET and pushed me beyond my comfort zone.

One of the most defining experiences of my undergraduate studies came during my junior year at BUET. Together with a teammate, I designed an electric-powered passenger vessel for Bangladesh. At the time, electric marine transportation was largely unexplored in the country. But I was motivated by the potential of sustainable technologies to reduce environmental impacts in inland waterways. The project results were published in the Journal of Maritime Research. But it exposed me to a practical challenge that later shaped my research direction. I discovered that the vessel's most persistent enemy was not its engine but its hull. A slow, invisible creep of hull biofouling increased drag, raised fuel consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, and shortened maintenance cycles. That single observation reshaped my research agenda: if I could teach a machine to see and classify what human inspectors struggle to locate reliably, I could convert a maintenance problem into an environmental solution. Thus I was motivated to investigate whether inspection and maintenance processes could be automated. I had no formal background in computer science. I taught myself deep learning from scratch, through self-directed study of online courses, tutorials, and research literature. For months, I ran training experiments on Colab and Kaggle GPUs late into the night, learning to read loss curves the way a mechanic listens to an engine. The effort culminated in a YOLOv8-based biofouling and corrosion segmentation system for my undergraduate thesis, using a freshly curated biofouling and corrosion dataset collected directly from Narayanganj Dockyard. The project also received the RISE Student Research Grant, which provided both funding and formal recognition that this self-directed work had scientific merit.

Successfully defending my thesis was, paradoxically, the moment my real research began. The system worked, but a nagging bottleneck remained: with only 35 high-resolution hull images in the dataset, every performance metric was sensitive to how augmentations were ordered and combined. A result that looked strong under one augmentation sequence could collapse under another. This was not a flaw I could footnote away. It was a fundamental challenge for any vision system deployed in data-scarce industrial environments. Rather than moving on, I stayed with the problem after graduation. I spent months designing a structured experimental framework. I analyzed 110 pairwise augmentation combinations. The result is a full research manuscript, available as a preprint on SSRN. I submitted it to several high-impact journals and used their reviewer feedback to strengthen the work substantially. I am currently addressing the remaining comments before resubmitting to a suitable venue. Working on this paper without institutional pressure, without a supervisor reminding me of deadlines, and without formal enrollment, purely out of intellectual compulsion; while also working as a materials-development instructor and continuing to tutor students to support myself, taught me something important about myself. I do not need external structure to do rigorous work. What I need is the right environment and the right collaborators, and the room to make this my full focus rather than something fit around paid work. 

However, my previous research focused primarily on enabling machines to perceive their environment through computer vision. But perception is only one component of an autonomous system. To create practical engineering solutions, it must be integrated with the rest of the system. This experience has sharpened my understanding of what applied computer vision can and cannot yet do reliably on its own. Moreover, it has also made clear what I do not yet know: the control loops, sensor fusion, planning, and actuation that turn a detection output into a decision or a physical action. This is precisely what I want graduate school to give me. I am not arriving with expertise in robotics or autonomous navigation. I am arriving with a demonstrated ability to do rigorous, self-directed applied research: hands-on experience conducting independent engineering research, from field-data collection and dataset development to model training, validation, and prototype implementation. These experiences taught me how to work under data scarcity, imperfect operating conditions, and real deployment constraints. I hope to contribute this application-oriented perspective to collaborative research projects while strengthening my expertise in robotics, autonomous systems, and intelligent sensing technologies.

In the short term, I aim to build the systems-level foundations I currently lack (control theory, sensor fusion, and autonomous navigation) that connect perception to physical action. In the long term, I intend to pursue doctoral research and contribute to the development of intelligent robotic platforms for infrastructure inspection, environmental monitoring, maritime applications, and disaster response. Ultimately, I aspire to become a research scientist or R&D engineer developing technologies that address real-world engineering challenges while supporting the transition toward safer, smarter, and more sustainable systems.
